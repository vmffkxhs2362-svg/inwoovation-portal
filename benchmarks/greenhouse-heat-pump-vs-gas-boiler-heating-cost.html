🔥 Thermodynamic Derivation: Carnot COP, Spark Spread & Bivalent Load Duration Curves

Decarbonizing commercial greenhouse heating requires replacing or supplementing fossil-fuel condensing boilers with industrial electrically driven heat pumps (utilizing low-GWP refrigerants such as $\text{NH}_3$ / R717, Propane R290, or HFO R1234ze). While a natural gas boiler converts chemical fuel energy into hydronic heat at a relatively static efficiency ($\eta_{\text{boiler}} \approx 0.92\text{–}0.96$ based on Lower Heating Value, or up to $1.04$ LHV when a secondary flue-gas condenser recovers latent water vapor enthalpy for low-temperature return rails), a vapor-compression heat pump upgrades low-grade ambient, geothermal, or aquifer thermal energy ($T_{\text{source}}$) to the greenhouse heating pipe supply temperature ($T_{\text{sink}}$).

The theoretical Lorentz/Carnot Coefficient of Performance ($COP_{\text{Carnot}}$) and real industrial Seasonal Coefficient of Performance ($SCOP$, or German Jahresarbeitszahl $JAZ$ per VDI 4650) are governed by the absolute temperature lift ($\Delta T_{\text{lift}} = T_{\text{sink}} - T_{\text{source}}$ in Kelvin):

COP_real = η_exergy × [ T_sink(K) / (T_sink(K) - T_source(K)) ]   (where η_exergy ≈ 0.48 – 0.58 for industrial screw/centrifugal compressors)

Because $COP$ degrades rapidly if hydronic supply temperatures exceed $55\text{ °C}$, modern heat-pump greenhouses enlarge heat-transfer surface area (installing finned under-gutter convectors, low-temperature $38\text{–}45\text{ °C}$ tube-rail loops, or overhead air-handling units) so the heat pump operates within its high-efficiency sweet spot ($SCOP = 3.6\text{–}4.5$).

2. Bivalent Sizing Strategy (40% Base-Load Capacity = 80% Annual Energy Coverage)

Designing an all-electric heat pump array to cover 100% of a greenhouse's 1-in-20-year polar vortex peak heating load ($200\text{–}260\text{ W/m}^2$) is economically suboptimal because the top 50% of peak thermal capacity operates for fewer than 180 hours per year. Instead, European and North American CEA engineers deploy a bivalent parallel architecture:

  • Base-Load Heat Pump (35–45% of Peak Load, ~70–90 W/m²): Operates 3,500–5,000 hours per year across autumn, spring, and moderate winter nights, supplying 78–86% of total annual gigajoules at SCOP 3.6–4.4.
  • Peak-Shaving Condensing Boiler or Thermal Buffer Tank (55–65% of Peak Load): Fires only during extreme sub-zero cold snaps (typically < 15% of annual thermal energy) and supplies supplementary CO₂ for daytime crop enrichment after flue-gas catalytic scrubbing.

3. Frequently Asked Questions (Thermal Decarbonization)

How do growers replace boiler flue-gas CO₂ when switching to electric heat pumps?
When natural gas boiler runtime drops by 80%, growers source photosynthetic CO₂ enrichment (800–1,000 ppm) from liquid food-grade CO₂ storage tanks (LCO₂), industrial carbon capture utilization (CCU) networks (such as OCAP in the Netherlands), or Direct Air Capture (DAC) modules integrated into greenhouse ventilation exhausts. Even after accounting for $0.18–$0.28/kg of purchased liquid CO₂ during high-PAR hours, bivalent heat pumps maintain a net OPEX advantage whenever SCOP exceeds 3.2.

Does a greenhouse heat pump also reduce summer cooling and dehumidification costs?
Yes. Reversible water-to-water heat pumps paired with cold/warm subterranean aquifer thermal energy storage (ATES) or fan-coil air treatment units (ATUs) chill greenhouse air during summer radiation peaks, condensing excess canopy transpiration vapor and storing the rejected sensible and latent heat underground for winter retrieval.
