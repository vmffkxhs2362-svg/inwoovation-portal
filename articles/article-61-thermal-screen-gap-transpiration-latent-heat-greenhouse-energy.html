Protected Horticulture & Thermodynamic Climate Control

Dynamic Thermal Screen Gap Control, Evaporative Transpiration Latent Heat & Psychrometric Energy Optimization in Semi-Closed Venlo Greenhouses

Published: 2026-09-12 • Author: Inwoo Hwang • Reading Time: 9 min

Heating energy constitutes up to 30% to 45% of total operating expenses in commercial cool-temperate greenhouse horticulture across Central Europe, North America, and Northeast Asia. Deploying movable interior thermal curtains (single or double aluminized polyester screens) reduces nocturnal envelope transmission losses by 35% to 50% under standard DIN V 18599 and EN 13031 engineering standards. However, closing an airtight screen traps dense moisture vapor continuously transpired by high-wire crops (tomatoes, sweet peppers, cucumbers). Within hours, interior relative humidity spikes past 90%, collapsing canopy Vapor Pressure Deficit (VPD < 0.2 kPa) and precipitating fungal pathogens (Botrytis cinerea) and blossom-end rot. Historically, growers counteracted humidity spikes by opening a 1% to 4% "screen crack" (screen gap) combined with minimum-pipe boiler heating. This article provides a rigorous physical analysis demonstrating that screen cracking induces destructive Rayleigh-Bénard buoyancy downdrafts that decimate thermal efficiency, and examines the psychrometric physics of modern active dehumidification with latent heat recovery (The Dutch Next Generation Growing / Het Nieuwe Telen paradigm).

1. The Thermodynamic Microclimate Conflict: Sensible Retention vs. Moisture Entrapment

A closed greenhouse canopy acts as an evaporative cooling engine. Solar radiation and supplemental LED/HPS lighting absorbed by vegetative tissues drive stomatal water vapor flux. Even during nocturnal dark periods, root pressure and residual metabolic activity maintain a continuous basal transpiration rate (Etrans):

Etrans, night ≈ 15 - 35 g·m-2·h-1 (0.15 - 0.35 mm/night)

In a standard 1-hectare (10,000 m2) Venlo facility, this basal transpiration injects between 150 and 350 liters of water vapor every hour into the air volume below the thermal screen. The total thermodynamic enthalpy (Hair, kJ/kg) of humid greenhouse air is partitioned into sensible and latent components:

h = cpa · T + W · ( hwe + cpw · T )

Where:

  • cpa: Specific heat capacity of dry air (1.006 kJ·kg-1·K-1).
  • T: Dry-bulb air temperature (°C).
  • W: Humidity ratio (mass of water vapor per unit mass of dry air, kgw/kgda).
  • hwe: Latent heat of vaporization of water at 0°C (2,501 kJ/kg).
  • cpw: Specific heat capacity of water vapor (1.86 kJ·kg-1·K-1).

Because the latent heat of vaporization is immense (2.45 MJ per kilogram of evaporated water), moisture accumulation represents a massive latent energy reservoir. When thermal screens are 100% closed, moisture cannot escape through roof vents. The air reaches saturation, dew condenses on the cold underside of the curtain and glass, and water droplets rain down upon crop heads, creating the ideal vector for sporulation.

2. Aerodynamics of the "Screen Gap" Fallacy: Rayleigh-Bénard Convective Inversion

To purge trapped moisture, traditional greenhouse climate computers automatically command a physical opening gap in the curtain drive rack (typically 15 to 40 mm, representing a 1% to 3% screen aperture). Simultaneously, the heating computer elevates heating rail pipe temperatures (45°C → 65°C) to drive buoyant air upward through the gap while exhausting moisture via cracked roof vents.

Fluid dynamics and thermal imaging demonstrate that this "screen gap" strategy is aerodynamically flawed and energetically catastrophic. The air attic space above the thermal curtain, directly adjacent to cold uninsulated roof glass (e.g., Tattic = 2°C to 6°C), is significantly denser than the warm canopy air below (Tcanopy = 18°C). The resulting density inversion creates intense Rayleigh-Bénard buoyancy instability:

Δ Pbuoyancy = g · (ρcold - ρwarm) · Δ h = g · ρmean · β · (Tcanopy - Tattic) · Δ h

Where β ≈ 1/Tmean is the thermal expansion coefficient of air (3.4 × 10-3 K-1 at 20°C), and Δ h is the vertical height from floor to screen (4.5 to 7.0 m). Rather than warm, moist air gently rising through the gap in a uniform laminar plume, the cold, heavy attic air plunges downward through the gap as a concentrated, high-velocity cold downdraft jet (vdown > 0.8 m/s).

Energy Loss from a 2% Screen Gap ≥ 35% - 50% of curtain savings forfeited

This localized cold jet descends directly onto the upper crop canopies located beneath the curtain drive truss lines. The localized shock creates severe microclimatic heterogeneity: leaf temperatures directly under the gap drop 3°C to 5°C below the ambient greenhouse target, bringing leaves below the dew point and inducing localized condensation—the very outcome the gap was intended to prevent.

3. The "Next Generation Growing" (Het Nieuwe Telen) Mathematical Paradigm

Pioneered by Wageningen University & Research (WUR) and leading Dutch greenhouse engineers, the Het Nieuwe Telen (NGW) methodology establishes that thermal screens should remain 100% closed without gaps throughout the entire heating demand cycle. Instead of relying on passive gap convection, climate control is separated into distinct, decoupled mechanical operations:

  1. Continuous Horizontal Air Circulation: High-efficiency, variable-speed EC circulation fans (or low-impulse diffuse air tubes positioned beneath crop gutters) operate continuously to homogenize temperature and prevent localized cold spots (vair ≈ 0.20 - 0.35 m/s).
  2. Vapor Flux through Screen Permeability: Modern high-performance curtains utilize vapor-permeable woven structures or micro-perforations that permit slow Fickian moisture diffusion (Jw = -Deff · ∇ C) while maintaining an airtight boundary layer that stops bulk convective exchange.
  3. Active Controlled Mechanical Dehumidification: Dedicated air handling units (AHUs) draw humid air from the canopy space, pass it across an evaporative cooling coil to condense water below its dew point, and immediately reheat the dry air via heat recovery before returning it to the greenhouse.

4. Psychrometric Enthalpy Recovery Calculation

Consider a 10,000 m2 Venlo greenhouse producing 250 kg/h of crop transpiration moisture at 18°C and 88% RH (W1 = 0.0114 kgw/kgda, enthalpy h1 = 47.0 kJ/kg). The design target is to lower humidity to 75% RH at 18°C (W2 = 0.0097 kgw/kgda, enthalpy h2 = 42.7 kJ/kg).

Under traditional screen crack ventilation, exhausting 250 kg/h of moisture requires venting 147,000 m3/h of warm air to the cold outdoors (-2°C, 90% RH, hout = 4.2 kJ/kg). The sensible and latent heating penalty imposed on the central boiler is:

Φloss, vent = ṁair · (h_1 - hout) = ( 176,400 kg/h / 3600 ) · (47.0 - 4.2) = 2,097 kW

In contrast, using an active mechanical heat-pump dehumidifier with internal sensible heat exchange, the condensation heat of water vapor (2,501 kJ/kg) is captured at the evaporator and rejected directly back into the dry supply air stream at the condenser:

Φrecovered = ṁcondensed · hwe = 250 kg/h × 2,501 kJ/kg / 3600 = 173.7 kW (Free Heat Return)

Operating with a heat pump Coefficient of Performance (COP ≈ 4.2), the electrical input to run the compressor is only 41.3 kW, while delivering 215 kW of net useful sensible heat into the greenhouse. Net fuel savings exceed 80% compared to minimum-pipe crack ventilation.

Engineering Summary & Meister Key Takeaways

Eliminating the traditional thermal screen gap is the single most effective operational upgrade in modern protected horticulture. By combining 100% closed double screens with active air circulation and mechanical heat-pump dehumidification, commercial growers eliminate cold downdrafts, secure uniform canopy VPD, and lower seasonal heating energy expenditures by 40% to 50% while completely preserving crop health.
